Executive summary

Growth enabled by the LMR Policy represents a significant step-change for Mosman, but one Council is well placed to plan for. This report summarises the analysis and research undertaken to identify existing infrastructure gaps and future needs of the community resulting from the expected additional 4,700 dwellings to be delivered within Mosman LGA.

The analysis summarised within this report shows that the existing population in Mosman is reasonably well serviced by infrastructure, notwithstanding the Military Road Spit Junction corridor operating at capacity. However, significant population growth expected from the LMR policy and associated development will put strain on existing infrastructure.

Additional community infrastructure will be required to service the population; this includes the redevelopment of the Barry O’Keefe Library into an integrated community hub, expanding the function and role of existing community facilities to make better use of existing assets for community programming and arts and culture services, the provision of additional indoor courts and exploration of opportunities to provide additional outdoor courts within existing assets. Additional open space will be required in the LGA, and it is recommended that council undertake further planning through the development of an open space strategy and acquisition plan. Council should consider opportunities for reclaiming land, expanding the carrying capacity of existing open space, and the provision of at least 0.5ha of passive open space within the masterplan area.

Expected growth in Mosman and the broader region will place increasing pressure on its already constrained transport network, and council is limited in its ability to address challenges associated with regional road corridors and public transport options. It is recommended that council focus on increasing pedestrian permeability and priority through the LGA, establishing a regional cycling route from the Spit Bridge through the LGA to North Sydney, reducing vehicle speeds where appropriate, improving public transport amenity, reducing off-street parking provision for new high-density development, alongside advocating the state government to improve bus service frequency and reliability and to explore new road connections including new east-west strategic transport links, additional Harbour crossing capacity, and upgrades to strategic interchanges.

Parking constraints will be addressed through the provision of parking within new residential and commercial buildings, additional parking infrastructure items and upgrades forming part of the infrastructure list, including the provision of electronic parking counters and considerations for the upgrade of existing single storey carparks into multi-storey. Stormwater infrastructure is to be further explored under Part 2 of the Mosman Flood Study, with consideration being given to likely future upgrades forming part of the infrastructure list. Consultation with Ausgrid and Sydney Water suggests that most utility impacts from LMR growth are manageable, though detailed assessment will be undertaken during the exhibition of the Mosman Masterplan.

Together, these findings provide the evidence base Council needs to finalise its infrastructure funding framework and to support its advocacy to the NSW Government for the infrastructure investment growth will require.

1. Introduction

1.1

Project background

Mosman Council faces a range of planning and infrastructure challenges arising from the NSW Government's Low and Mid-Rise Housing (LMR) Policy and related State-led housing initiatives. These reforms enable increased housing density across established residential areas and are expected to accelerate housing development through both the LMR Policy and State Significant Development (SSD) pathways. Council's primary concern is ensuring that infrastructure provision keeps pace with growth, so that Mosman's character, liveability, and service standards are maintained.

Why Mosman needs to understand infrastructure demand The LMR reforms could substantially increase dwelling yields across the municipality. While the theoretical capacity for additional housing may be high, Council needs to understand the likely rate and location of development uptake over the next 30 years to plan properly for infrastructure demand. The expected increase in population will add further pressure to both local and State-managed infrastructure networks.

Council has identified a need to assess how the LMR Policy will affect Mosman's infrastructure, including parking and traffic, stormwater and drainage, schools and education services, and health services and facilities.

The objective is to understand infrastructure needs for Council-owned assets and to identify broader State infrastructure needs that can support Council's advocacy with the NSW Government. Population growth generated through State policy must be matched by investment in infrastructure that supports both new and existing residents.

Why additional funding is required Mosman's current infrastructure contributions framework is unlikely to support the increased infrastructure demand from future housing growth. Council currently operates under a Section 7.12 Contributions Plan that applies a levy of 1% of estimated development cost. This framework provides limited capacity to fund the scale of infrastructure needed to accommodate growth generated by the LMR Policy.

A key challenge for Council is that State planning reforms may significantly increase development potential while infrastructure funding mechanisms remain largely unchanged. Without an updated funding framework, population growth could outpace infrastructure delivery, resulting in:

- increased congestion and parking pressure - greater demand on community facilities and open space - increased strain on drainage and stormwater systems - reduced accessibility to recreation, education, and health services

1.2

Policy context

Low and mid-rise Housing Policy The NSW Government's Low and Mid-Rise Housing (LMR) Policy is a State-led reform that aims to increase density in ‘well located’ regions across NSW. Stage 1 took effect on 1 July 2024, enabling dual occupancies across R2 zones statewide. Stage 2 commenced on 28 February 2025, applying within 800 metres of 171 town centres and stations. It enables low-rise housing (terraces, townhouses, low-rise apartments) on R1/R2 land and mid-rise housing (4–6 storey apartments) on R3/R4 land, with the greatest uplift within 400 metres of each centre. The controls override councils' height and density limits, though councils retain assessment powers over parking, solar access, and lot frontages. Heritage, bushfire-prone, coastal-sensitive, and employment or environmental living land is excluded.

In Mosman LGA, the policy applies to all appropriately zoned land within 800 metres of the Spit Junction and Cremorne town centres. The spatial extent of the policy is seen in the following image.

Mosman Council Masterplan Mosman Council at its meeting on 2 December 2025 resolved to prepare a long-term strategic Masterplan as a way of providing an alternate option to the LMR policy. 2.2

Forecast Dwellings

Council in conjunction with SJB and DPHI identified the additional dwelling capacity within Mosman LGA resulting from the LMR policy. This was done through identifying sites that were ‘available’ for development and excluding those that were unlikely to develop due to existing constraints, and using assumptions around dwelling yield and apartment size to determine a total likely floor area to be delivered, which was then converted into total additional dwellings.

It was determined that the LMR policy would result in additional capacity for 4,700 dwellings within the LGA (above existing controls). This number was used as the basis of forecasting the likely future population within the LGA.

2.3

Forecast population

Since 2016, Mosman LGA’s population has declined, reducing to 28,329 residents at the time of the 2021 Census. The estimated resident population in 2026 has slightly increased since 2021 to 28,755 residents.

Prior to the LMR policy announcement, the forecast for Mosman LGA was an additional 1,081 people from 2026 to 2051 (total of 29,836) (NSW Department of Planning, Housing and Infrastructure Population Projections). Since the introduction of the LMR, there is an increase in the forecast housing capacity for this area, with a dwelling capacity of 4,700 additional dwellings. Based on the housing typologies forecast (apartments), an average household size (i.e. persons per household) was applied to the additional housing capacity in the LMR. This results in an additional 9,050 people for the LMR catchment. This catchment, plus the broader LGA population results in a total population capacity of approximately 38,536 (Figure 5).

The build out rate of the LMR is unknown and will largely be driven by market demands and development opportunities. Given this, the dwelling capacity is not time bound. In assessing community infrastructure demands, understanding the full future capacity of growth in the LMR along with the broader municipality ensures that infrastructure needs are reflective of the total population growth across the LGA.

3.2

Open Space

Current Mosman's open space network includes a mix of pocket, local, district, and regional open space, but distribution across the LGA is uneven. The largest regional and conservation areas, including the headlands, sit to the south and south-east, while smaller local and pocket parks are dispersed more widely across the rest of the municipality. Around 91% of dwellings are within 400 metres of open space, falling to 88% once conservation land and the headlands are excluded, and much of this access is to small pocket parks with limited functionality rather than larger, multi-purpose spaces.

Sports fields illustrate a broader constraint. Mosman has 6 sports fields, mostly single ovals, several of which, including Balmoral Park, already support multiple codes across seasons to make the most of limited land. Asset condition is generally good to excellent, though utilisation ranges from around 37% of peak hours at Balmoral Oval to over 55% at Rawson, Middle Head, and Allan Border Ovals. Residents in the LGA's north and west currently fall outside a 5-minute drive of any sports field and rely on facilities in neighbouring LGAs.

The key existing constraint is land, not asset quality. Council has already increased the carrying capacity of several reserves through multi-sport use, lighting, and adjusted maintenance schedules, leaving limited additional capacity to unlock without acquiring new land, a genuinely scarce resource in a compact, largely built-out LGA.

Recommendations The Community Infrastructure Needs Assessment identifies a need for 2 additional outdoor sports fields and 4 additional local playgrounds, one of which should be located within the LMR catchment. Public open space across the LGA has limited scope for expansion, given the small size of the LGA and the lack of surplus land.

Recommended actions include:

- developing an Open Space Strategy that sets Council's future service standards for open space, open space functions, and supporting infrastructure such as playgrounds, dog parks, fitness equipment, and walking trails - developing an open space acquisition plan and actively working with developers and landowners to secure land, focusing on areas outside 400 metres of existing open space - exploring opportunities to reclaim land for public open space, such as roads, carparks, and end- of-life buildings - allocating at least a 0.5 hectare parcel of passive open space within the Master Plan area, ideally as a single parcel - increasing the carrying capacity of existing passive open space and sports reserves, including lighting upgrades and maintenance schedule changes at sites such as Reid Park, Spit West, and Georges Heights Oval - reviewing informal sports fields across the LGA to identify opportunities to host a more formal active sports role

3.3

Community Infrastructure

Current Mosman's community infrastructure network spans libraries, community centres and meeting spaces, youth services, arts and culture, aquatic facilities, and indoor and outdoor sporting courts. Provision centres on a small number of well-used, centrally located assets rather than a broad spread of facilities, and the network relies heavily on co-location and partnership access, including shared use of school facilities at Mosman High School and Queenwood School for Girls.

The Barry O'Keefe Library, the LGA's only library, records over 200,000 annual visits and continues to grow in popularity, but is reaching the end of its asset life and is no longer fit for purpose for contemporary library services. Similarly, the Marie Bashir Mosman Sports Centre provides the LGA's only publicly accessible multi-purpose indoor court, recording a peak-hour utilisation rate of 77% in 2025; the adjoining Drill Hall offers some supplementary space but cannot meet size requirements for sports such as netball and has restricted access.

Mosman is well serviced by arts and culture services, with the Mosman Art Gallery and Community Centre being well located, well utilised, and in good condition. Dedicated theatre and rehearsal space is limited to school facilities with restricted public access. Existing constraints are therefore less about asset quality and more about singularity and capacity, with most service types having only one facility covering the whole LGA, and there is limited scope for physical expansion given Mosman's land constraints.

Recommendations The Community Infrastructure Needs Assessment identifies the Barry O'Keefe Library as the priority community infrastructure response, noting Council has already identified this site for renewal within its Long Term Financial Plan (2025–2035).

The recommended response is to redevelop the library into an integrated community hub, including:

- a contemporary district library service, including consideration of 24-hour access to sections of the library - greater integration with youth services, while retaining a separate entry and exit for the youth centre - an arts rehearsal space with adequate storage, acoustics, flooring, technology, and soundproofing, incorporating a local theatre and performance space through flexible design where feasible, or an outdoor stage if this cannot be accommodated within the facility

3.4

Transport

Current Mosman's transport network is anchored by the Military Road – Spit Road corridor, one of the most critical regional links connecting the Northern Beaches to Sydney's CBD, North Sydney, and other employment centres. The corridor is physically constrained by the Spit Bridge and a series of signalised intersections, and functions as both a movement corridor and Mosman's principal activity centre at Spit Junction, where vehicle movement currently takes priority over pedestrian amenity. Around half of Mosman residents drive to work, close to a third use public transport, and a relatively high proportion work locally or from home compared with the Sydney average.

Buses are the dominant public transport mode, with 22 routes running along the corridor. The B-Line is the standout pressure point, where citybound B-Line services arriving at Spit Junction already operate at 100% occupancy and occasionally exceed theoretical capacity. Cycling infrastructure is comparatively underdeveloped, relying mainly on on-road, mixed-traffic conditions and the North Sydney network for onward trips to the CBD.

3.7

Utility and Agency Consultation

Ausgrid Consultation was undertaken with Ausgrid on 16th of July 2026. The purpose of the consultation was to discuss the forecast growth within Mosman LGA and how this was likely to impact existing Ausgrid infrastructure. The following points were raised during the discussion:

- Ausgrid expects minimal impact from most LMR development, as connections are typically handled site-by-site, with developers paying to connect to the 11kV network rather than requiring network upgrades. - Growth from the LMR Policy is largely already factored into Ausgrid's network planning through its forecasting work with the Department of Planning, Housing and Infrastructure. - The Mosman Masterplan has not yet been factored in, and Ausgrid has asked to review it during the masterplan's consultation phase, once the planning proposal is lodged, though development staging and timing remain difficult to predict given they will be market-driven. - Fast EV charging is the area of greatest potential impact, though this depends heavily on charger size and location. Smaller chargers can be readily accommodated, but DC fast chargers above 400kW may require network augmentation or extension, which is harder to deliver for individual developments than for larger sites such as shopping centres. Ausgrid suggests identifying strategic locations able to absorb this additional capacity and cost. It is noted that Mosman Council provided a Masterplan briefing to Ausgrid in May 2026 and further to this, the planning proposal for the masterplan will be formally referred to Ausgrid for comment.

Community infrastructure principles

Community infrastructure can be in place for 50-100 years and beyond. The delivery of services and community needs will continue to change and evolve over this time. Table 43: Guiding principles for the delivery of community services and infrastructure Principles Overview

Responds to the needs of the community ▪ Community infrastructure is service led, rather than asset led. ▪ The hierarchy, infrastructure, and operation of services respond to local needs. ▪ Community services and infrastructure consider the wider community infrastructure network providing complementary services and spaces.

Integration and co-location ▪ Through design and planning, community infrastructure should support and enable the co-location and integration of services. ▪ Complementary services should be integrated and or co-located within the same facility or site to support sharing of services and resources, improve service efficiencies, and provide an improved user experience. Accessibility and inclusivity ▪ Community services should be well located and accessible (where possible by car, public transport, and active transport). ▪ Community infrastructure should be safe and welcoming for all genders, cultures, ages, and abilities.

Partnership ▪ Partnerships with others to build, operate, and maintain community infrastructure should be considered. This could include joint use agreements with schools, leasing of spaces, use of air rights, state, federal government, or private/NFP market led infrastructure.

The NSW Government Low and Mid Rise (LMR) Housing Policy encourages higher density and more diverse housing development in town centres across NSW, including Mosman.

In the Mosman LGA, the policy applies to land surrounding the Spit Junction and Cremorne centres, enabling significantly greater density than permitted under local planning controls.

The policy represents a significant land use change with the potential to influence travel demand and network performance. While the policy is intended to concentrate growth in accessible locations, there has been limited publicly available assessment of the cumulative transport impacts associated with the additional housing capacity enabled at the local level.

This issue is particularly relevant for Mosman and surrounding communities on the Northern Beaches and Lower North Shore, where access is heavily dependent on the constrained Military Road – Spit Road . Additional residential growth has the potential to increase demand across all transport modes, including private vehicles, buses, walking and cycling.

In its part, Mosman Council is carrying out an infrastructure impact study to identify the local infrastructure required to support growth, test and refine different local contributions funding mechanisms, and develop an updated Contributions Plan to enable the delivery of local infrastructure.

Mosman Council is working with the NSW Government to prepare a long-term strategic Mosman Masterplan in response to the LMR Policy.

It has been agreed that the master plan’s residential development capacity must be equivalent to or greater than the LMR Policy- enabled capacity of 4,700 new dwellings.

The community-led approach will develop local controls and redistribute density with consideration to Mosman’s unique character, making sure that growth lines up with transport, schools, open space, and services.

The Masterplan proposes two development options:

• ‘Low and wide’ spreads lower-rise changes more gradually across a wider area around the Spit Junction

• ‘High and narrow’ concentrates taller buildings along the Military Road – Spit Road corridor, preserving more areas unaffected by the changes.

The Masterplan options were open for community feedback in May 2026. A draft Masterplan will be reported to the Council in August to September 2026.

The Northern Beaches Network Review is a strategic transport study undertaken by Transport for NSW to assess the performance of the transport network serving the Northern Beaches and Lower North Shore and to identify opportunities for improvement. An analysis has been undertaken to understand the nature of road crashes along the Military Road – Spit Road corridor, which is the area where the highest housing growth is planned.

Over five years, between 2020 and 2024, a total of 102 road crashes were recorded along the corridor section within Mosman. Key observations are:

• The Spit Junction is the most prominent crash cluster, especially at Ourimbah Road (6 crashes). Other crash clusters are identified at or around Warringah Road (8 crashes), Central Avenue (7), and Lang Street (6).

• Rear-end collisions were most common (31 crashes, or 30%), followed by lane-change (12 crashes), and head-on collisions (6 crashes).

• Out of 10 crashes that involved pedestrians, overwhelmingly 8 crashes occurred around the Spit Junction.

The analysis shows that road crashes along the corridor can be attributed to traffic congestion, which often leads to drivers’ and pedestrians’ impatience.

Any additional traffic along the corridor will add to road safety issues. Future interventions and management need to consider safety for all road users, especially vulnerable road users, including pedestrians, cyclists, seniors, and people with limited mobility.

Significant growth is planned along the Military Road – Spit Road corridor. The LMR Policy would enable 4,700 new dwellings in Mosman, while North Sydney and the Northern Beaches are targeted to add 11,450 and 12,000 new dwellings by 2036, respectively, in their LGAs.

Without publicly available State-led modelling and assessment, this study attempts to illustrate the potential impact of housing growth on uplift in car and bus trips at a high level.

This can be estimated at a high level through trip generation calculation based on the Transport for NSW (2024) Guide to Transport Impact Assessment.

Adopting a conservative approach, using the travel patterns of high-density residential development, yields 0.19 car trips and 0.22 bus trips per dwelling per hour during the AM peak.

Based on Census data, it is further assumed that 80% of future Mosman residents and 50% of future Northern Beaches residents would travel in the citybound direction along the Military Road – Spit Road corridor to go to work.

This results in up to 1,480 additional cars and 1,720 additional bus trips along the corridor in the citybound direction during the AM peak hour by 2036.

The total uplift in car trips almost doubles the existing volumes along the corridor in 2024–2025, which sat around 1,200 to 1,600 vehicles per hour.

This amount of additional traffic will exceed the capacity of the corridor and lead to extensive delays and a significant increase in travel time towards North Sydney and Sydney CBD.

Additionally, the uplift in bus trips would fill up all existing bus capacity while requiring additional bus services. The estimated uplift translates to about 22 buses based on a capacity of 80 spaces per bus.

It is crucial to note that this high-level estimation hasn’t taken into account new developments in the North Sydney LGA, particularly in Neutral Bay and Cremorne. The uplift in traffic volumes generated by these growths will put strain on the downstream of Military Road towards North Sydney and the Warringah Freeway, which will have a knock-on effect on traffic on the upstream of the corridor in Mosman and the Northern Beaches.

A regional challenge

Housing growth in Mosman and across the Northern Beaches and Lower North Shore corridor will place increasing pressure on an already constrained transport network.

The challenge is regional in nature: the Military Road – Spit Road corridor is a critical regional transport link between the Northern Beaches, Mosman, North Sydney, and Sydney CBD. Its constraints are largely driven by regional travel demand, limited alternative routes, and constrained harbour crossing capacities.

The corridor is physically constrained and highly sensitive to small increases in traffic. Previous State investigations found that an additional 500 vehicles in the peak direction could increase corridor travel times by up to 60%, from 28 minutes to 45 minutes.

The LMR Policy would enable thousands of homes in the region, resulting in up to 1,480 additional car trips and 1,720 public transport trips along the corridor during peak periods—beyond its practical operating capacity.

Limited local government capacity

Local governments are responding to State housing targets to support walking, cycling, public transport amenity, safer streets, and better place outcomes on the local roads and places through targeted local interventions.

For Mosman, Council is preparing the Mosman Masterplan and associated studies to identify opportunities to deliver housing capacity and the local infrastructure required to support this. However, a combined response is needed to support the scale of growth now expected across the region.

Local governments have limited funding capacity and jurisdiction over the State-managed transport network, bus services and major infrastructure investments needed to address corridor-wide transport challenges.

Mosman Council, through its submission to the Northern Beaches Network Review, advocates for a shift toward more ambitious, regionally significant transport outcomes and a step-change in infrastructure investment.

NSW State Government

The NSW Government should therefore take a leading role in identifying, funding, and delivering the enabling infrastructure required to support its housing objectives.

Without substantial State intervention, local improvements will be unable to offset the cumulative impacts of additional housing growth, increased car trips, and rising demand for public transport, and will fail to meaningfully deliver good Place and liveability outcomes.

The study finds that the transport infrastructure and services currently available within the Mosman LGA will be insufficient to meet the transport needs of the expected uplift.
